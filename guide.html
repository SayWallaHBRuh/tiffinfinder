Tiffin 101: how to choose a tiffin service in Calgary

What a tiffin service is

A tiffin (also called a dabba) is a home-style meal, usually cooked that day, carried or delivered in a stacked metal lunchbox — the tiffin carrier or dabba itself is typically two to four steel tiers that lock together with a handle. A tiffin service is a home kitchen or small caterer that cooks a rotating menu and sends it out to the same households on a regular schedule, instead of a one-off restaurant order.

The word “tiffin” came into English in colonial India in the 19th century for a light midday meal, and the tiffin carrier became a common way for office workers to bring lunch from home. The best-known version of the idea is Mumbai’s dabbawala system: a home-lunch delivery network started in 1890 by Mahadeo Havaji Bachche with about a hundred workers, later formalised as a trust in 1956. Today it moves an estimated 175,000 to 200,000 lunch boxes a day, carried mostly by bicycle and train from homes to offices and back. Calgary’s tiffin kitchens work on the same basic idea — a household’s own cooking, on a schedule — adapted to pickup and delivery instead of a citywide courier network.

How plans usually work

Most tiffin kitchens sell a few plan lengths rather than single meals only:

Daily

Pay per meal, the most flexible and usually the most expensive per meal.

Weekly

Five weekday meals is the common pattern, at a per-meal discount over daily.

Monthly

The deepest discount, for households who know they’ll stick with it.

Trial week

A short, no-commitment week some kitchens offer before you sign up for longer, often priced close to their normal weekly rate.

A typical tiffin meal is a home-style thali: rice or roti (flatbread) as the base, a dal (lentils), one or two sabzi (cooked vegetable dishes), and sometimes a side like pickle, yogurt or a sweet. How many rotis you get, whether rice is included every day, and whether a side changes daily are worth asking about directly — they vary kitchen to kitchen and aren’t always spelled out on a menu.

Source: Thali, Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

On price: what a kitchen charges is up to that kitchen, and Tiffin Finder doesn’t set or collect any of it. In September 2026, publicly advertised Calgary tiffin plans we found ranged roughly $10–$24 a day, $70–$90 a week and $240–$320 a month, with trial weeks typically priced close to a kitchen’s normal weekly rate. Prices vary a lot by kitchen, portion size and what’s included — not a quote you can hold anyone to — so always ask each kitchen directly for its current price before you order.

Pickup means you collect the meal from a spot the kitchen has chosen to share (its precision is the kitchen’s choice — an exact address, a nearby intersection, or just the neighbourhood); delivery means the kitchen (or a driver working for it) brings it to you, usually within a set list of areas. Some kitchens offer both. Ask which days a plan actually covers — “weekly” doesn’t always mean every weekday, and most kitchens pause for public holidays.

Tiffin service vs. meal prep vs. meal kits

These three get lumped together because they all promise “stop cooking most nights” — but they work differently, and the difference is mostly who cooks, and when.

Tiffin service

A kitchen — a home cook, caterer, restaurant or commissary cook — cooks a rotating home-style menu and sends it out already cooked, on a repeating schedule (daily, weekly or monthly), for pickup or delivery. You reheat if you want it hot; you don’t cook it.

Meal prep

Meals are cooked in batches ahead of time and portioned into containers to eat across the week — sometimes done by a household itself, sometimes bought as a service. Like a tiffin, the food arrives fully cooked; meal-prep services are usually sold by the container and often lean into calorie or macro counts rather than a rotating home-style menu.

Meal kit

A company ships pre-portioned, often raw or partly-prepared ingredients plus a recipe card, and you do the cooking at home. This is the one real difference from the other two: with a meal kit, nobody has cooked your dinner yet.

So a tiffin service sits closest to meal prep — both hand you already-cooked food on a schedule — and furthest from a meal kit, which hands you ingredients and a recipe, not a finished meal.

How to choose a kitchen

  1. 1

    Spice level

    “Home-style” spice varies a lot between households and regions. Ask whether the kitchen can go mild, and whether that’s a standing note or something you have to repeat every order.

  2. 2

    Veg, halal, Jain and allergens

    Confirm dietary needs directly with the kitchen rather than assuming from the cuisine or name — “vegetarian” and “Jain” aren’t the same thing, and allergen handling (like nuts or dairy) is worth a direct question if it matters to your household.

  3. 3

    Portion size and roti count

    A portion that works for one adult may be light for two, or too much for a child. Ask how many rotis come with a meal and whether rice is separate or included.

  4. 4

    Delivery or pickup days

    Check which days of the week are actually covered, what happens on statutory holidays, and how far in advance you need to pause or skip a day.

  5. 5

    Containers and returns

    Some kitchens use reusable steel tiffins you return and rinse; others use disposable containers. Ask which, and if reusable, how the swap works — most households find this easier to plan for up front than to figure out on day one.

  6. 6

    Whether it’s taking new customers

    A kitchen with a waitlist or that’s full right now may still be worth asking about for a later start date. A trial week, where offered, is a low-commitment way to check the fit before a longer plan.

  7. 7

    Allergens

    Health Canada lists 11 priority food allergens: eggs, milk, mustard, peanuts, crustaceans and molluscs, fish, sesame seeds, soy, sulphites, tree nuts, and wheat and triticale. A home-style kitchen often cooks several dishes in the same pots, with shared spices, ghee or nuts on hand — worth a direct question if anyone in your household has a serious allergy, since “no X on the menu” isn’t the same as “no X in the kitchen.”

  8. 8

    Jain and no-onion-no-garlic prep

    “Vegetarian” doesn’t mean Jain. Jain home cooking leaves out onion, garlic and root vegetables (potato, carrot and similar), and some households also avoid food cooked after sunset. If this matters to you, ask specifically: does the kitchen leave out onion, garlic and root vegetables, when was today’s food cooked, and are the same pans and oil used for other, non-Jain dishes?

Questions to ask before you start

A short list to have ready the first time you message a kitchen:

  • What plans do you offer, and what’s included in a typical meal (roti count, rice, sides)?
  • Can you do mild / less spicy, and is that a standing note on my account?
  • Is the menu vegetarian, halal, Jain, or does it vary by day — and can you handle my allergy or dietary need?
  • Which days do you deliver or have pickup, and what happens on holidays?
  • Do you use reusable containers or disposable ones, and how does a swap or return work?
  • Do you offer a trial week, and what does it cost?
  • How do I pause, skip a day, or cancel, and how much notice do you need?
  • Do you have a current AHS Food Handling Permit for the kitchen you cook in? (See how permits work.)

Food-safety basics worth checking

You don’t need to be an expert to ask a few sensible questions. Here’s what an official Canadian source actually says, so you know what “safe” means in practice:

Ask about

  • The permit. Does the kitchen have a current AHS Food Handling Permit for an approved kitchen? Tiffin Finder checks this for every listing and shows the date it was checked. That’s a permit check, not a food-safety inspection or endorsement. Details: How permits work.
  • Labelling. A straightforward kitchen can tell you what’s in a meal and roughly when it was cooked, so you can judge freshness and check for anything you’re allergic to.
  • How it’s kept cold in transit. Meals that sit at room temperature for a while before pickup or delivery are worth a question about how they’re kept cold until you get them.

What Health Canada says, for your own kitchen

  • The “danger zone” where bacteria grow quickly is 4°C (40°F) to 60°C (140°F). Keep cold food at or below 4°C and hot food at or above 60°C.
  • Refrigerate leftovers within two hours of cooking to limit bacterial growth.
  • When reheating, heat food to at least 74°C (165°F), and bring soups, gravies and sauces to a full rolling boil.
  • Set your fridge to 4°C (40°F) or lower and your freezer to -18°C (0°F) or lower.

Sources: Health Canada, Food safety and you (opens in a new tab) · Health Canada, Safe food storage (opens in a new tab)

A permit check is not a food-safety inspection or endorsement. It confirms a permit exists and is current — it says nothing about a specific meal. If something seems off with a meal, don’t eat it, and tell the kitchen. Urgent food-safety concerns can go to AHS Environmental Public Health at 1-833-476-4743.

Is a home tiffin kitchen legal in Calgary?

Short answer: a home kitchen that cooks and sells hot tiffin meals in Alberta needs an AHS Food Handling Permit for an approved kitchen, plus City of Calgary business licensing and location approval. The small home-food exemption a lot of people have heard of doesn’t cover hot tiffin meals — it’s meant for things like baked goods and candies, not a daily dal-and-rice plate. How permits work has the full process, fees and every source, kitchen-facing; this is the short, household-facing version.

  • Alberta’s Food Regulation exempts only “low-risk home-prepared food”: food that doesn’t need refrigeration (baked goods, candies, whole fresh produce, some canned goods) and contains no meat, poultry, seafood or unpasteurized milk. It can be sold from home, online or at a farmers’ market, but must be labelled.
  • A hot tiffin meal — rice or roti, dal, a cooked vegetable, often meat — needs temperature control and commonly isn’t low-risk, so it isn’t covered by that exemption.
  • Selling food that needs temperature control from a home means an AHS Food Handling Permit for an approved kitchen, plus a City of Calgary Food Service – Premises business licence and, for cooking in a home, a Home Occupation development permit (Class 1 or Class 2, depending on how the kitchen is set up).
  • Tiffin Finder checks and shows the date checked for every real kitchen’s permit before it’s listed, and never lists one without it. Sample kitchens are always labelled “Sample.”

Choosing a balanced tiffin

You don’t need a nutrition panel to eat well — a few simple habits from Canada’s food guide go a long way, and they work whether or not a kitchen has posted any numbers.

  • Think in quarters. Canada’s food guide suggests filling about half your plate with vegetables and fruit, a quarter with whole grains, and a quarter with protein foods — including plant-based ones like beans, lentils and tofu more often. A tiffin thali (dal, a sabzi, rice or roti, and a side) can fit this shape reasonably well; a kachumber salad or extra sabzi is an easy way to round it out.
  • Whole grains, where you can. Whole-grain roti, brown rice or millets count for more than refined ones. Worth a quick ask if it matters to you — most kitchens can tell you what flour or rice they use.
  • Go easy on sodium. Highly processed and heavily salted foods tend to run higher in sodium, added sugars and saturated fat. If a kitchen’s food tends to be salty for your taste, say so — most will adjust.
  • Oil and ghee are part of home cooking, in moderation. Tempering (tadka), frying and ghee are normal in South Asian and many other home-style cuisines. If you’re watching how much oil or ghee is in your food, ask the kitchen rather than guessing.
  • Water first. Canada’s food guide recommends water as the drink of choice over sugary drinks.

These are general public-education points, not a rating of any kitchen’s food — no kitchen on Tiffin Finder is scored or ranked against them.

Sources: Canada’s food guide — the plate model, Health Canada (opens in a new tab) · Canada’s Dietary Guidelines, section 1, Health Canada (opens in a new tab)

What a kitchen’s nutrition panel means (and its limits)

Some kitchens choose to share an estimated calorie and protein range for a meal, and an allergen “Contains:” list, on their own page. This is entirely optional — most won’t, and that’s fine, since restaurant-style meals like tiffins aren’t required to carry a nutrition label the way a packaged product is.

When a kitchen does share it, a few things are worth knowing:

  • It’s the kitchen’s own estimate, usually worked out from a typical recipe — not a lab test. Home-style batch cooking varies week to week (a bit more oil, a different vegetable ratio), so figures are shown as ranges, never a single exact number.
  • Tiffin Finder doesn’t check, test or verify any of these figures, the same way we don’t inspect the food itself. If something looks off, ask the kitchen directly, or use the “Report a problem” link on that kitchen’s page.
  • An allergen tag is a courtesy from the kitchen, not a promise. Home and shared kitchens often cook several dishes with shared pots, oil and spices, so cross-contact is common and hard to rule out completely — always confirm directly with the kitchen if an allergy matters to your household.
  • We keep kitchens to plain, factual wording here (things like “vegetarian” or “mild spice”). We don’t allow claims that a dish is low in a specific nutrient, a fit for a medical diet, or free of a specific allergen, unless a kitchen can back that up — those are the kind of claims Health Canada and the CFIA regulate closely, and they’re easy to get wrong in a home kitchen, so we’d rather a kitchen say nothing than say something it can’t stand behind.

Sources: Nutrition labelling for restaurants and food-service businesses, CFIA (opens in a new tab) · Food allergen labelling, Health Canada (opens in a new tab)
