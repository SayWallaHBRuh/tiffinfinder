Tiffin 101: how to choose a tiffin service in Calgary

A plain-language guide for households new to tiffin: what a tiffin service is, how plans and prices usually work, how to choose one, what to ask a kitchen before you start, and the food-safety basics worth checking. General information, not advice — every claim below links to where it came from.

What a tiffin service is

A tiffin (also called a dabba) is a home-style meal, usually cooked that day, carried or delivered in a stacked metal lunchbox — the tiffin carrier or dabba itself is typically two to four steel tiers that lock together with a handle. A tiffin service is a home kitchen or small caterer that cooks a rotating menu and sends it out to the same households on a regular schedule, instead of a one-off restaurant order.

The word “tiffin” came into English in colonial India in the 19th century for a light midday meal, and the tiffin carrier became a common way for office workers to bring lunch from home. The best-known version of the idea is Mumbai’s dabbawala system: a home-lunch delivery network started in 1890 by Mahadeo Havaji Bachche with about a hundred workers, later formalised as a trust in 1956. Today it moves an estimated 175,000 to 200,000 lunch boxes a day, carried mostly by bicycle and train from homes to offices and back. Calgary’s home tiffin kitchens work on the same basic idea — a household’s own cooking, on a schedule — adapted to pickup and delivery instead of a citywide courier network.

How plans usually work

Most home tiffin kitchens sell a few plan lengths rather than single meals only:

  • Daily — pay per meal, the most flexible and usually the most expensive per meal.
  • Weekly — five weekday meals is the common pattern, at a per-meal discount over daily.
  • Monthly — the deepest discount, for households who know they’ll stick with it.
  • Trial week — a short, no-commitment week some kitchens offer before you sign up for longer, often priced close to their normal weekly rate.

A typical tiffin meal is a home-style thali: rice or roti (flatbread) as the base, a dal (lentils), one or two sabzi (cooked vegetable dishes), and sometimes a side like pickle, yogurt or a sweet. How many rotis you get, whether rice is included every day, and whether a side changes daily are worth asking about directly — they vary kitchen to kitchen and aren’t always spelled out on a menu.

Source: Thali, Wikipedia (opens in a new tab)

On price: what a kitchen charges is up to that kitchen, and Tiffin Finder doesn’t set or collect any of it. As a sense of scale only — not a quote you can hold anyone to — the sample listings on this site range from about $11–$15 a day, $60–$90 a week and $205–$320 a month, with trial weeks typically priced close to a kitchen’s normal weekly rate. Always confirm the current price with the kitchen itself before you order.

Pickup means you collect the meal from a spot the kitchen has chosen to share (its precision is the kitchen’s choice — an exact address, a nearby intersection, or just the neighbourhood); delivery means the kitchen (or a driver working for it) brings it to you, usually within a set list of areas. Some kitchens offer both. Ask which days a plan actually covers — “weekly” doesn’t always mean every weekday, and most kitchens pause for public holidays.

How to choose a kitchen

  1. 1

    Spice level

    “Home-style” spice varies a lot between households and regions. Ask whether the kitchen can go mild, and whether that’s a standing note or something you have to repeat every order.

  2. 2

    Veg, halal, Jain and allergens

    Confirm dietary needs directly with the kitchen rather than assuming from the cuisine or name — “vegetarian” and “Jain” aren’t the same thing, and allergen handling (like nuts or dairy) is worth a direct question if it matters to your household.

  3. 3

    Portion size and roti count

    A portion that works for one adult may be light for two, or too much for a child. Ask how many rotis come with a meal and whether rice is separate or included.

  4. 4

    Delivery or pickup days

    Check which days of the week are actually covered, what happens on statutory holidays, and how far in advance you need to pause or skip a day.

  5. 5

    Containers and returns

    Some kitchens use reusable steel tiffins you return and rinse; others use disposable containers. Ask which, and if reusable, how the swap works — most households find this easier to plan for up front than to figure out on day one.

  6. 6

    Whether it’s taking new customers

    A kitchen with a waitlist or that’s full right now may still be worth asking about for a later start date. A trial week, where offered, is a low-commitment way to check the fit before a longer plan.

Questions to ask before you start

A short list to have ready the first time you message a kitchen:

  • What plans do you offer, and what’s included in a typical meal (roti count, rice, sides)?
  • Can you do mild / less spicy, and is that a standing note on my account?
  • Is the menu vegetarian, halal, Jain, or does it vary by day — and can you handle my allergy or dietary need?
  • Which days do you deliver or have pickup, and what happens on holidays?
  • Do you use reusable containers or disposable ones, and how does a swap or return work?
  • Do you offer a trial week, and what does it cost?
  • How do I pause, skip a day, or cancel, and how much notice do you need?
  • Do you have a current AHS Food Handling Permit for the kitchen you cook in? (See how permits work.)

Food-safety basics worth checking

You don’t need to be an expert to ask a few sensible questions. Here’s what an official Canadian source actually says, so you know what “safe” means in practice:

Ask about

  • The permit. Does the kitchen have a current AHS Food Handling Permit for an approved kitchen? Tiffin Finder checks this for every listing and shows the date it was checked. That’s a permit check, not a food-safety inspection or endorsement. Details: How permits work.
  • Labelling. A straightforward kitchen can tell you what’s in a meal and roughly when it was cooked, so you can judge freshness and check for anything you’re allergic to.
  • How it’s kept cold in transit. Meals that sit at room temperature for a while before pickup or delivery are worth a question about how they’re kept cold until you get them.

What Health Canada says, for your own kitchen

  • The “danger zone” where bacteria grow quickly is 4°C (40°F) to 60°C (140°F). Keep cold food at or below 4°C and hot food at or above 60°C.
  • Refrigerate leftovers within two hours of cooking to limit bacterial growth.
  • When reheating, heat food to at least 74°C (165°F), and bring soups, gravies and sauces to a full rolling boil.
  • Set your fridge to 4°C (40°F) or lower and your freezer to -18°C (0°F) or lower.

Sources: Health Canada, Food safety and you (opens in a new tab) · Health Canada, Safe food storage (opens in a new tab)

A permit check is not a food-safety inspection or endorsement. It confirms a permit exists and is current — it says nothing about a specific meal. If something seems off with a meal, don’t eat it, and tell the kitchen. Urgent food-safety concerns can go to AHS Environmental Public Health at 1-833-476-4743.
